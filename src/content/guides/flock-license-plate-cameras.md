**Last checked: October 2026** · Product claims below are what Flock's own pages say, with a link. Lawsuits and accusations are allegations unless a ruling or audit finding is named. City decisions change monthly, so check your own town. This is not legal advice.

Flock says it has more than 120,000 license plate cameras across 49 states ([Flock](https://www.flocksafety.com/blog/the-price-of-free-lpr)). There is no way to opt out of being photographed. Flock says it can't process privacy requests (access, deletion or correction) for camera images, and the data belongs to whoever owns the camera ([Flock privacy notice](https://www.flocksafety.com/legal/privacy-policy)).

What you can do is find out how your town uses them. One question covers it:

**How long does my town keep the data, and who is allowed to search it?**

The answers differ a lot from town to town. This guide shows you where to look.

## What a Flock camera records

### Plates, and more

A license plate reader (ALPR, for automated license plate reader) is a camera that photographs passing cars and turns the plate into searchable text.

Flock says its cameras capture plate images, vehicle characteristics, date and time, and camera location ([Flock](https://www.flocksafety.com/trust/data-privacy)). Its product page says the cameras record "make, color, and body type," and that features called FreeForm and Vehicle Signature can "recognize thousands of unique vehicle characteristics" ([Flock](https://www.flocksafety.com/products/license-plate-readers)).

An October 2026 essay by a criminology professor describes this as capturing "distinguishing marks down to dents or a bumper sticker" ([Fortune/The Conversation](https://fortune.com/2026/10/03/flock-cameras-cbp-access-data-sharing/)). That means a car can be found even without a full plate.

### What it says about people

Flock says the system "does not use facial recognition" and that "It captures vehicles. Not people." ([Flock](https://www.flocksafety.com/trust/data-privacy)).

In September 2026, WIRED and 404 Media analyzed data that hackers had copied from one Flock camera. About 21 days of logs held roughly 50,000 vehicles and 1.6 million images. The camera's software is built to detect people and bicycles as well as cars, though none of the analyzed images showed pedestrians ([SAN](https://san.com/cc/hackers-stole-a-flock-camera-heres-what-they-found-inside/)). Both outlets said they found no evidence of facial recognition. Flock said the unauthorized removal of a camera is illegal ([SAN](https://san.com/cc/hackers-stole-a-flock-camera-heres-what-they-found-inside/)).

So the software is built to notice that a person is there. The reporters found no evidence that it recognizes who they are.

### How to recognize one

Look for:

- **A slim pole**, often head height, sometimes strapped to an existing post ([DeFlock Wilmington](https://deflockilm.org/how-to-spot-a-flock-camera/)).
- **A small solar panel on top** and a boxy black camera below it ([DeFlock Wilmington](https://deflockilm.org/how-to-spot-a-flock-camera/)).
- **Aimed at the backs of cars.** Plate readers "almost always face the rear of vehicles," and many give off a faint red infrared glow at night ([DeFlock](https://deflock.me/identify)).

They sit at intersections, on-ramps, and the entrances to neighborhoods, shopping centers and parks ([DeFlock Wilmington](https://deflockilm.org/how-to-spot-a-flock-camera/)). At a glance they look like a speed sign or traffic sensor.

### How many there are

Flock says it has "more than 120,000 cameras across 49 states" and more than 12,000 customers, including neighborhoods, cities, counties and businesses ([Flock](https://www.flocksafety.com/blog/the-price-of-free-lpr)). Flock has said about 5,000 agencies have contracted with it ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/08/13/1141904/flock-is-tightening-its-rules-in-response-to-a-growing-surveillance-backlash/)).

## How long it's kept and who owns it

### Often 7 days, but it depends on your town

Flock's Evidence Policy (last updated August 12, 2026) says plate reader data is kept in the cloud for seven days "and then permanently deleted," unless the law or the customer's contract says otherwise ([Flock Evidence Policy](https://www.flocksafety.com/legal/flock-evidence-policy)).

That 7-day default is new. Until August 2026 it was 30 days. Flock's CEO said existing customers keep their current setting ([Flock](https://www.flocksafety.com/blog/flock-guardrails-address-lpr-privacy-concerns-and-police-transparency)). Departments can also choose to override it ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/08/13/1141904/flock-is-tightening-its-rules-in-response-to-a-growing-surveillance-backlash/)).

Flock says most communities keep 7 days ([Flock](https://www.flocksafety.com/trust/data-privacy)). Your town's number may differ, so check its transparency portal (see below). A new "Evidence Mode," free for agencies that adopt the 7-day default, lets detectives save specific records as evidence for an active case ([Flock](https://www.flocksafety.com/blog/flock-guardrails-address-lpr-privacy-concerns-and-police-transparency)). State law can also set the limit (see the table below).

### The customer owns it

Flock says "Customers retain ownership and control of all data" ([Flock Evidence Policy](https://www.flocksafety.com/legal/flock-evidence-policy)). The customer is the police department, HOA or business that bought the camera.

That's why you can't get your car deleted. Flock's privacy notice (August 2025) says the footage doesn't connect to an identifiable person, so it is "unable to process data subject requests related to such images" ([Flock privacy notice](https://www.flocksafety.com/legal/privacy-policy)).

Flock says the data is encrypted in transit and at rest on Amazon Web Services ([Flock Evidence Policy](https://www.flocksafety.com/legal/flock-evidence-policy)).

## Who can search it

### Local, statewide, national

Flock says customers set their own sharing, and "Nothing is shared unless they choose to share it" ([Flock](https://www.flocksafety.com/trust/data-privacy)). Its sharing options include:

- **1:1 sharing** with other departments, by radius or across the state.
- **National Lookup**, which lets an agency search a full plate across every other agency that opted in. Flock said in June 2025 that National Lookup is turned off in California ([Flock](https://www.flocksafety.com/blog/statement-network-sharing-use-cases-federal-cooperation)).

### Opt-in on paper, and what one city found

Mountain View, California, found its cameras shared far more than it expected. As Malwarebytes reported, "statewide lookup" was active on 29 of its 30 cameras for 17 months, and more than 250 agencies ran an estimated 600,000 searches in a single year. National Lookup was on for one camera, and the city says Flock turned it on without telling it. The police department said Flock kept no records from the national lookup period, so it can't tell what data left the system. The city shut off all 30 cameras on February 3, 2026 ([Malwarebytes](https://www.malwarebytes.com/blog/privacy/2026/02/flock-cameras-shared-license-plate-data-without-permission)).

### HOAs and stores

Police aren't the only owners. Flock says an HOA owns its footage, and police get access only if the HOA opts in ([Flock](https://www.flocksafety.com/industries/hoas)).

Businesses can share too. Records obtained by EFF and reported by 404 Media in 2025 showed a Texas sheriff's office could search Flock cameras at 173 Lowe's stores nationwide and at dozens of Home Depot stores in Texas ([404 Media](https://www.404media.co/home-depot-and-lowes-share-data-from-hundreds-of-ai-cameras-with-cops/)).

## What has gone wrong

These are the main reported problems, with what was found kept apart from what is alleged.

**Federal access in Illinois (state official's audit, 2025).** In August 2025, Illinois Secretary of State Alexi Giannoulias said an audit found Flock broke state law by letting US Customs and Border Protection (CBP) access Illinois camera data through a pilot. He ordered the access cut off. Flock acknowledged it "didn't create distinct permissions and protocols" ([NBC Chicago](https://www.nbcchicago.com/news/local/company-violated-illinois-law-by-sharing-license-plate-data-with-customs-and-border-protection-giannoulias/3814914/)) but disagrees with his legal conclusions ([Capitol News Illinois](https://capitolnewsillinois.com/news/hundreds-of-police-departments-use-camera-company-accused-of-breaking-state-law/)).

**Immigration searches through local police (reporting, 2025).** 404 Media reported that local and state police ran more than 4,000 lookups for federal agencies or with a possible immigration focus, with reasons typed in like "ICE" and "immigration." ICE had no Flock contract. The searches went through local departments ([404 Media](https://www.404media.co/ice-taps-into-nationwide-ai-enabled-camera-network-data-shows/)). 404 Media later reported that CBP had searched more than 80,000 Flock cameras through a pilot ([404 Media](https://www.404media.co/cbp-had-access-to-more-than-80-000-flock-ai-cameras-nationwide/)).

Flock confirmed it ran "limited pilots" with CBP and Homeland Security Investigations. It said it paused all federal pilots and that federal users would no longer be added to statewide or nationwide lookups. It said any sharing request from a federal agency will be clearly marked as federal ([Flock](https://www.flocksafety.com/blog/ensuring-local-compliance)).

**California sues El Cajon (allegation, 2025).** In October 2025, Attorney General Rob Bonta sued the city of El Cajon, alleging it shared plate data with out-of-state agencies in violation of state law ([California AG](https://www.oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-sues-el-cajon-illegally-sharing-license-plate-data-out)). No ruling has been reported.

**The Texas abortion search (records, 2025).** In May 2025, a Texas officer searched more than 83,000 Flock cameras nationwide, including in states where abortion is legal, for a woman who had self-managed an abortion ([404 Media](https://www.404media.co/a-texas-cop-searched-license-plate-cameras-nationwide-for-a-woman-who-got-an-abortion/)). Flock said she was searched for "as a missing person" ([Flock](https://www.flocksafety.com/blog/statement-network-sharing-use-cases-federal-cooperation)). The search log gave the reason as "had an abortion, search for female." In October 2025, EFF published court records, including the lead detective's sworn affidavit, describing a death investigation opened after a report of the abortion. EFF says this contradicts the missing-person account ([EFF](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2025/10/flock-safety-and-texas-sheriff-claimed-license-plate-search-was-missing-person-it)).

**Officers tracking partners (accusations and charges, 2026).** A Washington Post investigation has identified at least 100 police department employees charged with or accused of misusing plate readers, including officers who tracked current or former partners ([Fortune/The Conversation](https://fortune.com/2026/10/03/flock-cameras-cbp-access-data-sharing/)). Senator Josh Hawley's letter cites a Milwaukee officer charged after allegedly searching his girlfriend's plate 124 times, entering "investigation" as the reason each time. The letter says he was caught because a private citizen looked up his own plate on a public website ([Hawley letter](https://www.hawley.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/2026-08-26-Hawley-Letter-to-Flock-CEO.pdf)).

**Flock's changes (August 2026).** Flock announced the 7-day default. It said that by the end of 2026 it will require misuse detection for all police customers and a case number for every search, with an emergency bypass. It also lets a city block other agencies from searching its cameras for some purposes, such as immigration enforcement ([Flock](https://www.flocksafety.com/blog/flock-guardrails-address-lpr-privacy-concerns-and-police-transparency)). MIT Technology Review noted Flock won't verify the case numbers officers enter ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/08/13/1141904/flock-is-tightening-its-rules-in-response-to-a-growing-surveillance-backlash/)). The ACLU said the retention cut "might constitute a positive development" but that most other changes treat privacy concerns "as mere public relations problems" ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/14/flock-adds-safeguards-to-licence-plate-readers-after-wave-of-stalking-cases)).

**Senate investigation (August and September 2026).** Senator Hawley, who chairs a Senate Judiciary subcommittee, opened an investigation into Flock's collection, retention and sharing of data. He wrote that the company's internal policies are "in practice, the only safeguards for hundreds of millions of Americans" ([Hawley letter](https://www.hawley.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/2026-08-26-Hawley-Letter-to-Flock-CEO.pdf)). At a subcommittee hearing on September 23, Flock's CEO declined to testify ([Roll Call](https://rollcall.com/2026/09/23/flock-cameras-draw-bipartisan-concerns-at-senate-hearing/)). On September 30, Hawley introduced the Stop Flock Abuse Act. It would ban local governments from selling plate reader data and require deleting it after 10 days unless it is tied to an active investigation ([KCUR](https://www.kcur.org/politics-elections-and-government/2026-09-30/josh-hawley-flock-automated-license-plate-readers)).

## What the law says

The rules come mostly from states and from each customer's own policy. Some states set their own limits:

| State | How long data can be kept | Other limits | Law |
|---|---|---|---|
| New Hampshire | 3 minutes, unless tied to an arrest, citation, or wanted or missing person | Plate records may not be recorded or transmitted anywhere; readers may not photograph the people inside the car | [RSA 261:75-b](https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/xxi/261/261-75-b.htm) |
| Virginia | 21 days; audit logs 2 years | No sharing with other states', federal, private or commercial databases, with listed exceptions; misuse is a misdemeanor | [Va. Code 2.2-5517](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title2.2/chapter55.6/section2.2-5517/) |
| Maine | 21 days, with exceptions | Data is confidential; only listed government agencies may use readers | [29-A MRSA 2117-A](https://legislature.maine.gov/statutes/29-A/title29-Asec2117-A.html) |
| Oregon | 30 days, unless tied to a case | People can sue vendors that break the law | [Oregon Laws 2026 ch. 77](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/lawsstatutes/2026orLaw0077.pdf) |
| California | No statewide limit | Police may share only with other California public agencies | [Civ. Code 1798.90.55](https://california.public.law/codes/civil_code_section_1798.90.55), [AG bulletin](https://oag.ca.gov/system/files/media/2023-dle-06.pdf) |

**Washington** passed SB 6002 in March 2026. Flock's own summary says it sets 21-day retention, bans immigration enforcement use, and bars collection near places like hospitals, schools and places of worship ([Flock](https://www.flocksafety.com/blog/what-does-washington-senate-bill-6002-mean-for-alpr-use-in-washington)).

**Illinois** bars sharing plate data for immigration enforcement or abortion investigations. Capitol News Illinois notes the law has no specific penalties ([Capitol News Illinois](https://capitolnewsillinois.com/news/hundreds-of-police-departments-use-camera-company-accused-of-breaking-state-law/)).

California lawmakers have tried and failed five times since 2022 to tighten plate reader rules. The governor vetoed a 2025 bill partly over its 60-day deletion rule, and the 2026 bill died on the last day of session ([CalMatters](https://calmatters.org/economy/technology/2026/09/california-kills-flock-camera-regulation-again/)).

### In court

- **Oklahoma, October 1, 2026.** A federal judge in Oklahoma ruled that one deputy's search of a driver's month of plate history was "an Unconstitutional Warrantless Search" and threw out the evidence. The ruling applies to that one criminal case. Flock said it expects the ruling to be appealed and overturned ([404 Media](https://www.404media.co/federal-judge-rules-a-flock-search-was-indiscriminate-mass-surveillance-and-unconstitutional/)).
- **Norfolk, Virginia.** In January 2026 a federal judge ruled for the city in a challenge to its 176 Flock cameras, writing that plate readers could become too intrusive "at some point," but "at least in Norfolk, Virginia, the answer is: not today" ([WHRO](https://www.whro.org/business-growth/2026-02-11/a-federal-judge-ruled-norfolks-flock-surveillance-cameras-dont-invade-peoples-privacy-yet)). The appeal is pending. The court has tentatively set argument for December 8 to 11, 2026, but on September 15 the city told the court it is available on none of those days, so the date may change ([UNC School of Government](https://nccriminallaw.sog.unc.edu/2026/09/14/quick-thoughts-about-flock-cameras-the-fourth-amendment-and-the-courts/), [CourtListener docket](https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/72357963/lee-schmidt-v-city-of-norfolk/)).
- **San Jose, California, October 2026.** A state judge dismissed a lawsuit that said the city's warrantless police searches of its plate reader data violate the California Constitution ([San José Spotlight](https://sanjosespotlight.com/judge-hands-san-jose-win-in-license-plate-reader-case/)).
- **Washington records.** In November 2025 a Skagit County judge ruled Flock images are public records ([Cascadia Daily](https://www.cascadiadaily.com/2025/nov/13/skagit-judge-rules-license-plate-reader-images-are-public-record/)). Stanwood and Sedro-Woolley later settled for $80,000 after a judge found they had wrongfully withheld footage ([Everett Herald](https://www.heraldnet.com/2026/08/14/stanwood-sedro-woolley-settle-flock-records-case-for-80k/)). Separately, SB 6002, signed in March 2026, exempted plate reader data from public records requests ([MRSC](https://mrsc.org/stay-informed/mrsc-insight/april-2026/license-plate-reader-data)).

## What you can do

### 1. Find the cameras near you

**DeFlock** is a free, volunteer-built map of plate readers, stored in OpenStreetMap ([DeFlock](https://deflock.me)). If you spot a missing camera, you can add it with an OpenStreetMap account ([DeFlock](https://deflock.me/report/id)).

The map only shows what volunteers have reported. Coverage is uneven and cameras move. One local DeFlock group tested cheap radio scanners and found they detected none of the cameras it had confirmed by eye. It concluded that "public records and visual identification with the DeFlock app remain the dependable methods" ([DeFlock Wilmington](https://deflockilm.org/map-the-cameras-yourself/)).

### 2. Check your town's transparency portal

Many agencies publish a Flock Transparency Portal. Flock says over 1,500 have ([Flock](https://www.flocksafety.com/blog/flock-guardrails-address-lpr-privacy-concerns-and-police-transparency)). A portal can show the agency's policy, retention period, who it shares with, and a search log with case numbers and reasons ([Flock](https://www.flocksafety.com/blog/how-flock-builds-transparency-into-public-safety-technology)).

The address usually looks like transparency.flocksafety.com/city-state-pd, as in Shaker Heights, Ohio's portal ([Shaker Heights](https://www.shakerheightsoh.gov/1045/Flock-Safety-Transparency-Portal)). Search the web for "[your city] Flock transparency portal" or check your police department's site.

Portals are optional. If your town doesn't have one, you can ask for one.

### 3. Request the records

A public records request asks a government agency for documents it holds. DeFlock suggests asking the police department for contracts and invoices, and the city for installation permits, which often include a map of locations. It notes many states let you file anonymously ([DeFlock](https://deflock.me/foia)).

Useful records to ask for:

- The signed Flock contract and any amendments
- The agency's plate reader policy
- The list of agencies it shares with
- The search audit log

Audit logs matter most. Newburgh, New York's posted logs showed its data was searched thousands of times by agencies around the country, including the abortion-related search ([WAMC](https://www.wamc.org/news/2026-09-29/newburgh-city-council-votes-to-end-flock-contract)).

In Washington, the images are now exempt, but audit logs can still be requested, with some identifiers redacted ([MRSC](https://mrsc.org/stay-informed/mrsc-insight/april-2026/license-plate-reader-data)). Virginia exempts both the data and the audit logs from its public records law, though agencies must post yearly reports on how they used the system ([Va. Code 2.2-5517](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title2.2/chapter55.6/section2.2-5517/)). Maine makes the data confidential ([Maine](https://legislature.maine.gov/statutes/29-A/title29-Asec2117-A.html)).

### 4. Go to the council meeting

Contracts come up for renewal, and councils vote on them. Cities that ended or chose not to renew Flock include:

- **Austin, Texas** (June 2025), after the city manager pulled the renewal vote ([KUT](https://www.kut.org/austin/2025-06-04/austin-tx-automatic-license-reader-program-police-data-privacy-flock))
- **Denver, Colorado** (February 2026), which switched to another vendor, Axon, with 21-day retention ([Colorado Politics](https://www.coloradopolitics.com/2026/02/24/denver-ends-contract-with-license-plate-camera-vendor-flock/))
- **Asheville, North Carolina** (August 2026), which ran 11 cameras. Another 86 in the city, run by others including HOAs and the school system, stayed on ([Carolina Public Press](https://carolinapublicpress.org/80468/asheville-terminates-flock-contract-in-heated-council-meeting/))
- **Lynchburg, Virginia** (September 2026), at the end of its term ([WSLS](https://www.wsls.com/news/local/2026/09/03/lynchburg-flock-camera-resolution/))
- **Newburgh, New York** (September 2026) ([WAMC](https://www.wamc.org/news/2026-09-29/newburgh-city-council-votes-to-end-flock-contract))
- **Henrico County, Virginia** (October 2026), whose police division is terminating its contract over privacy concerns. The board chair said the county "cannot ignore the potential for misuse of system data by others" ([Henrico Citizen](https://www.henricocitizen.com/breaking-henrico-cancels-flock-safety-contract/))

Others kept them. Dallas's council rejected a proposal to cut $1.5 million and remove its remaining Flock cameras ([CBS Texas](https://www.cbsnews.com/texas/news/dallas-city-council-vote-flock-cameras-privacy-data-concerns/)). After Texas paused state funding, Flock agreed to keep Dallas's 321 state-funded cameras running for the next 90 days ([CBS Texas](https://www.cbsnews.com/texas/news/dallas-police-flock-cameras-status-9-30-2026/)). Flock says the cities that left are a small share of its 5,000 agencies ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/08/13/1141904/flock-is-tightening-its-rules-in-response-to-a-growing-surveillance-backlash/)).

Ask about the contract term too. Lynchburg's council weighed a $35,000 early-termination fee ([WSLS](https://www.wsls.com/news/local/2026/09/03/lynchburg-flock-camera-resolution/)).

### 5. Don't cover your plate

Plate covers and sprays that block cameras are illegal in many states. California fines $250 per violation ([Cal. Veh. Code 5201.1](https://california.public.law/codes/vehicle_code_section_5201.1)). Texas fines up to $300 for a covering that obscures the plate, rising for repeat offenses ([Tex. Transp. Code 504.945](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._transp._code_section_504.945)).

## FAQ

**Can I opt out of Flock cameras?**

No. Flock says it can't process privacy requests (access, deletion or correction) for camera images because the footage isn't linked to an identifiable person ([Flock privacy notice](https://www.flocksafety.com/legal/privacy-policy)). The data belongs to the camera's owner, so changes have to come through your town's policy or state law.

**How long does Flock keep my data?**

It depends on the customer. Flock's default since August 2026 is seven days, after which it says the data is permanently deleted ([Flock Evidence Policy](https://www.flocksafety.com/legal/flock-evidence-policy)). Customers who signed earlier keep their old setting ([Flock](https://www.flocksafety.com/blog/flock-guardrails-address-lpr-privacy-concerns-and-police-transparency)). The old default was 30 days. Check your town's transparency portal for its number.

**Is it legal to cover my license plate?**

In many states, no. California and Texas both ban covers that hide a plate from cameras ([Cal. Veh. Code 5201.1](https://california.public.law/codes/vehicle_code_section_5201.1), [Tex. Transp. Code 504.945](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._transp._code_section_504.945)).

**Can ICE search Flock cameras?**

ICE has no Flock contract, but 404 Media reported local police ran thousands of searches for federal agencies, some with reasons like "ICE" or "immigration" ([404 Media](https://www.404media.co/ice-taps-into-nationwide-ai-enabled-camera-network-data-shows/)). Flock says it paused federal pilots and stopped adding federal users to statewide and national lookups ([Flock](https://www.flocksafety.com/blog/ensuring-local-compliance)). Some states, such as California, Illinois, Virginia and Washington, restrict this kind of sharing (see the law section above).

**Does Flock use facial recognition?**

Flock says no ([Flock](https://www.flocksafety.com/trust/data-privacy)). Reporters who analyzed data from a hacked camera found its software is built to detect people, but found no evidence of facial recognition ([SAN](https://san.com/cc/hackers-stole-a-flock-camera-heres-what-they-found-inside/)).

**How do I find Flock cameras near me?**

Check the DeFlock map, which volunteers keep up to date in OpenStreetMap ([DeFlock](https://deflock.me)). It may miss some cameras. For your town's own cameras, ask your police department or city for the contract, invoices and installation permits ([DeFlock](https://deflock.me/foia)).
