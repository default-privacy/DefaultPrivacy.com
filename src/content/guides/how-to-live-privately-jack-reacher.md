**Published 2026-10-09 · General information, not legal advice**

Jack Reacher, the drifter in Lee Child's novels, owns a folding toothbrush and not much else: no address, no phone, no car. That makes him a useful lens on privacy, because nearly everything he does leaves less behind. Most of his life isn't legal or practical for a real person, which is why every habit below comes with a second half: the legal version you can copy this week.

*Default Privacy is not affiliated with or endorsed by Lee Child, his publishers or the Reacher TV series. We use the character as commentary.*

## The habits

### 1. He carries almost nothing

**What Reacher does:** A folding toothbrush and the clothes he's wearing. After 9/11 he added a passport and an ATM card, and in *Bad Luck and Trouble* the card lives inside the passport.

**The legal version you can copy this week:** Empty your wallet onto a table. Your Social Security card goes back in a drawer at home. The Social Security Administration's own advice is "do not routinely carry your card." Cards you haven't used in a month stay home too. On your phone, set a real screen lock, and move passwords out of your notes app into a password manager such as [Bitwarden](/directory/bitwarden).

**Why it matters:** Whatever you carry is what you can lose. A lost phone with no lock is a lost wallet with every account attached.

### 2. He throws his old clothes away

**What Reacher does:** Every few days he buys a cheap outfit and puts the old one in a bin. In *Bad Luck and Trouble* an old army friend points out he could have kept the shirt. Reacher calls it a "slippery slope": a spare shirt leads to a suitcase, then a house. Lee Child has called the habit "rigorously rational."

**The legal version you can copy this week:** Do the same with old accounts. Run your email through the [breach check](/breach-check) to see which forgotten services have already leaked it, then close the ones you no longer use. [JustDeleteMe](/directory/justdeleteme) collects direct deletion links for a long list of sites.

**Why it matters:** An account you stopped using still holds your name, your email and sometimes a card or an address. It can still be breached.

### 3. He fills out as few forms as he can

**What Reacher does:** The same scene ends with what he is actually avoiding: a life of filling out "all kinds of forms" (*Bad Luck and Trouble*). Every form is a record with his name on it.

**The legal version you can copy this week:** You can't skip the forms the law requires. You can stop the copies. Opt out of prescreened credit and insurance offers at [OptOutPrescreen](/directory/opt-out-prescreen), the official opt-out run by the credit bureaus: five years online, permanent by mail. Then work through the people-search sites with [the data broker removal guide](/guides/remove-name-from-data-brokers), or let [Permission Slip](/directory/permission-slip) send opt-out and deletion requests for you. DeleteMe bought the app in July 2026, and the free version still sends requests.

**Why it matters:** Forms become records, and records become profiles that someone else sells.

### 4. He has no address

**What Reacher does:** In *Killing Floor*, the first novel, a detective asks for his address and he says he has none. The jacket copy for *Bad Luck and Trouble* sums him up the same way: no phone, no ties, no address.

**Not workable for a real person.** Most states want a residential address for a driver's license, voter registration needs the place you live, and banks are required to collect a street address. Putting a mailbox where the law asks for your home gets applications refused, and a false address on official forms is a problem of its own.

**The legal version you can copy this week:** Keep your real address on the records that require it, and stop giving it out anywhere else. A virtual mailbox such as [Anytime Mailbox](/directory/anytime-mailbox) gives you a street address for shops and subscriptions, and scans your mail so you can read it from anywhere. It is fully above board, and it isn't anonymous. The provider checks a photo ID and proof of your home address, keeps your signed USPS Form 1583 on file and enters it in a Postal Service database. The Postal Service releases that record only under a subpoena or court order. The provider's staff also handle your mail: they photograph each envelope, and open and scan the contents when you ask. Then scrub the copies already online with [Keep your home address off public records](/guides/keep-home-address-off-public-records).

If you are a survivor of domestic violence or stalking, many states run an address confidentiality program that gives you a substitute address for public records. Ask your secretary of state or attorney general's office.

**Why it matters:** Your home address is the one piece of data that puts a stranger on your doorstep.

### 5. He pays cash

**What Reacher does:** In *Killing Floor* the police go through his belongings and find no credit cards. By *Bad Luck and Trouble* he lives on ATM withdrawals from a single bank account in Virginia.

**Legal, up to a point.** Paying cash is legal. Paying cash for everything isn't practical: many hotels, car rental desks and most online checkouts want a card. Large amounts don't go unnoticed either. Banks report cash transactions over $10,000, businesses report cash payments over $10,000, and splitting a payment to stay under that line is a federal crime even when the money is clean.

**The legal version you can copy this week:** Use cash where it's easy, for small purchases in person. Online, use a virtual card from [Privacy.com](/directory/privacy-com) that only works at one merchant, so a breach at one store doesn't expose the card you use everywhere. Privacy.com still verifies who you are and your bank still sees the charges. What it hides is your real card number, and it hides it from the store.

**Why it matters:** One card number used at fifty stores is fifty copies waiting for a breach.

### 6. He doesn't carry a phone

**What Reacher does:** "No phone" is right there in the *Bad Luck and Trouble* jacket copy. When he needs to call his bank in that book, he finds a phone in a bus depot.

**Legal, and close to impossible now.** Pay phones are mostly gone, and banks, employers and two-factor logins all assume you have a phone.

**The legal version you can copy this week:** Keep the phone and trim what it gives away. Turn off location access for apps that don't need it. On an iPhone, switch off Allow Apps to Request to Track; on Android, delete or reset the advertising ID in your privacy settings. On Android, [TrackerControl](/directory/trackercontrol) shows which apps talk to trackers. For shops and sign-ups, hand out a second number from [MySudo](/directory/mysudo) instead of your real one. MySudo phone numbers need a paid plan.

**Why it matters:** A phone number is a lookup key. Apps and data brokers use it to tie your accounts and records together.

### 7. He gives his name and very little else

**What Reacher does:** In *Killing Floor* the detective gets a first name, a last name, no middle name and no address. Reacher doesn't lie to the police about who he is. He just doesn't volunteer anything extra.

**The legal version you can copy this week:** Give shops, apps and newsletters an email alias instead of your real address. [SimpleLogin](/directory/simplelogin), [Addy](/directory/addy) and [Firefox Relay](/directory/firefox-private-relay) forward alias mail to your inbox, and you can switch off any alias that starts getting spam. Police, courts, banks, employers and the tax office get your real details. Aliases are for sign-ups.

**Why it matters:** One email address used everywhere links your accounts across breaches and broker files. A different alias per site breaks the link.

### 8. He uses a different name at the front desk

**What Reacher does:** In *One Shot* he pays cash for a hotel room under the name of an old Yankees second baseman. He had run through the vice presidents years before.

**Don't copy the front-desk part.** A hotel that asks for ID gets your real name.

**The legal version you can copy this week:** Newsletters, sign-ups and store loyalty programs have no right to your legal name. Give them a nickname or a made-up name, paired with an email alias, so a leak of their customer list doesn't carry your real identity. Give your real name wherever ID or the law requires it: hotel check-in, banks, employers, landlords, police and government offices. Never give a false name to anyone legally entitled to the real one.

**Why it matters:** Your full name is what data brokers match everything else against. Every list that pairs it with your email is one more record they can join.

### 9. He watches the one trail he keeps

**What Reacher does:** In *Bad Luck and Trouble* he checks his bank balance against a running total in his head, every time. That is how he spots an extra $1,030. It's a message from Frances Neagley, from his old army unit. In the book, 10-30 is the military police radio code for a colleague who needs urgent help. The one account he kept is how she found him.

**The legal version you can copy this week:** Pick the accounts everything else depends on, your main email and your bank, and lock them. Give each a unique password, and let a password manager such as [Bitwarden](/directory/bitwarden) generate it for you. Turn on two-factor login, and switch on transaction alerts at the bank. In the US, freezing your credit at Equifax, Experian and TransUnion is free and stops most new credit being opened in your name; the [FTC explains how](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-credit-freezes-and-fraud-alerts). The recovery steps on the [breach check](/breach-check) cover the rest.

**Why it matters:** Your inbox is your version of Reacher's bank account. Whoever controls it can reset everything else.

### 10. He looks at a setup the way a hostile stranger would

**What Reacher does:** In *Without Fail*, a Secret Service agent hires him to find the holes in the protection around the Vice President-elect. He and Neagley find several.

**The legal version you can copy this week:** Run a small version on yourself. Search your name and town and see what comes up. Before you give a site your details, put it through the [privacy scanner](/scanner) to see its trackers and cookies. If you own a domain, the [WHOIS check](/whois) shows whether your name and address sit on the registration, and the [privacy score](/privacy-score) checks the domain in one pass.

**Why it matters:** You can't fix exposure you haven't looked at.

## Where Reacher's way breaks down for real people

**A stranger with no trail is easy to blame.** In *Killing Floor*, Reacher is arrested for murder within an hour of walking into town, because the police chief falsely claims he saw him leave the scene. A drifter with no address and no local ties makes a convenient suspect. Even Reacher carries a passport and a bank card in the later books.

**The law wants an address.** Licenses, voter rolls, banks and courts need the real one. A mailbox address is for everyone else.

**Cash stops at the counter.** Hotels, rentals and the internet run on cards, and large cash transactions get reported whatever you do.

**His privacy costs him a home, a car, a job and most of his relationships.** That trade works on paper. Lee Child told Men's Journal he tried living like Reacher for a few weeks and found it "exhausting."

**The trail he kept found him anyway.** One bank account was enough for Neagley.

Reacher gets his privacy by owning almost nothing. You get yours by deciding what you hand out, and to whom.

## FAQ

**Is it legal to live like Jack Reacher?**

Parts of it. Owning very little, paying cash for small things and going without a smartphone are all legal. Having no address on records that require one, or moving large sums in cash to avoid the reports, is either unworkable or against the law.

**Is a virtual mailbox legal?**

Yes. The provider has to collect USPS Form 1583, a photo ID and proof of your home address before it accepts your mail. Use it for shops and subscriptions. Your license, voter registration and bank still get your home address.

**Can I pay cash for everything?**

You can try, but many hotels, rental desks and most online stores want a card, and cash over $10,000 gets reported. Online, a merchant-locked virtual card is the practical middle ground.

**Will these steps make me invisible?**

No. They cut down what strangers, shops and data brokers can collect about you. They don't hide you from your bank, the IRS or a court with valid legal process, and they aren't meant to.

**Which step matters most?**

Locking down your main email. It is the account that resets all your other accounts, which makes it your version of Reacher's one bank account.

**Is this based on the books or the TV series?**

The books. The first two seasons of the Amazon series adapt *Killing Floor* and *Bad Luck and Trouble*, so most of these habits carry over, though the show changes some details.
